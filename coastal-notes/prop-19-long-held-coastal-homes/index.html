Prop 19 and Long-Held Coastal Homes: Parent-Child Transfers in Encinitas

Families who have owned an Encinitas home for decades often carry a very low assessed value. Prop 19 narrowed the parent-child exclusion, and an appraisal is often central to calculating what can still be protected.

Key points

  • Since February 16, 2021, the exclusion covers only a family home the child occupies as a principal residence.
  • Protection extends only as far as the factored base year value plus one million dollars, adjusted for inflation.
  • Market value on the transfer date sets how much of the old base survives.
  • File form BOE-19-P and the homeowners' exemption on time with the County Assessor.
  • Confirm details with an estate attorney, CPA or the Assessor before deadlines pass.

What changed on February 16, 2021

Prior to Proposition 19, Proposition 58 let California parents transfer a primary residence plus substantial other property to their children with no reassessment. For transfers made on February 16, 2021 or later, Prop 19 took the place of that system. The parent-child exclusion now applies only to a family home, meaning the parent's principal residence, and only when a child makes it his or her own principal residence. Rentals, vacation homes and most other real property transferred between parents and children are reassessed to current market value. Similar rules apply to transfers from grandparents to grandchildren in limited circumstances. For coastal families who bought in Encinitas, Cardiff or Leucadia in the 1970s or 1980s, the difference between the old Prop 13 base and current market value can be enormous, which makes the rules and the valuation date worth getting right.

How the value cap works

Even when a child qualifies, the exclusion is capped. The child retains the parent's factored base year value only when the home's market value at transfer does not exceed that factored base year value by more than one million dollars. That one-million-dollar figure is adjusted for inflation every two years by the State Board of Equalization. When market value exceeds the limit, the excess is added to the factored base year value to produce the new taxable value. For a long-held coastal house, this is where the numbers matter. A home with a very low inherited base and a high current market value may keep part of its tax benefit, but the amount depends directly on the market value as of the transfer date. Families should confirm current figures and their own calculation with the County Assessor or a tax professional.

Why an independent appraisal matters

The San Diego County Assessor will set a market value for the transfer, but families are not required to accept an estimate without support. A well-documented appraisal as of the date of transfer, which is usually the date of death for inherited property, gives the family and its advisors a clear basis for understanding the reassessment and, where appropriate, for discussing it with the Assessor or pursuing an assessment appeal. Long-held homes often have dated kitchens, original systems, deferred maintenance or unpermitted additions that a mass-appraisal estimate may not capture. An appraiser inspects or reviews those conditions and selects sales of similar homes in similar condition near the transfer date. The same report can also serve as evidence of stepped-up income tax basis for the heirs, which reduces duplicated effort and keeps the values consistent.

Timing: moving in and filing the claim

Two deadlines drive most Prop 19 family-home transfers. The child must move in and make the property a principal residence, and must file for the homeowners' exemption or the disabled veterans' exemption within one year of the transfer to receive the exclusion from that date. The claim for reassessment exclusion is filed on Board of Equalization form BOE-19-P with the County Assessor. Generally, the claim should be filed within three years of the transfer or before the property is transferred to a third party, and a late claim may receive relief only going forward. When siblings inherit together and only one moves in, the exclusion and its calculation can become more involved. Because rules and deadlines carry real consequences, families should confirm their specific situation with an estate attorney, a CPA or the Assessor's office before deadlines pass.

Valuation issues specific to coastal homes

Coastal Encinitas creates particular challenges for Prop 19 valuations. Homes west of Interstate 5 can differ sharply in value block by block, depending on ocean views, walking distance to Moonlight Beach or Swami's, lot depth and whether a property sits in the coastal zone with added permit limits. A small 1950s cottage on a large Leucadia lot may derive most of its value from land, while a remodeled house a few streets away trades on its finishes. An appraiser studies sales near the transfer date that match the subject's location, view and condition, then explains any adjustments. If the family plans to remodel after moving in, the valuation still reflects the home as it existed on the transfer date. Clear photos and records from before any changes help preserve that evidence for the Assessor and for future questions.

Questions

Does Prop 19 apply to a rental property inherited from a parent?

Since February 16, 2021, a vacation or rental property transferred from a parent to a child is usually reassessed at market value, as the parent-child exclusion now applies solely to a parent's primary residence that the child also lives in. An appraisal as of the transfer date helps the heirs understand the new assessed value and establishes stepped-up basis. An attorney or CPA can confirm how the rules apply to a specific estate.

What date should a Prop 19 appraisal use?

The effective date is normally the date of transfer. For inherited property, that is usually the date of death. For a transfer during the parent's lifetime, it is typically the date the deed is recorded or the transfer occurs under the trust or deed terms. The Assessor's office or an estate attorney can confirm the correct date, and the appraisal should state it clearly so the value lines up with the claim.
