Central San Diego

Old Town home appraisals

San Diego's historic birthplace, with hillside homes and transit-area condos

Old Town sits where Mission Valley opens out, close to the spot where the San Diego River formerly met the bay, beneath the hill on which the Presidio and first mission were established in 1769. The Mexican-era pueblo grew around the plaza that is now part of Old Town San Diego State Historic Park. After the 1870s, commercial life moved to Alonzo Horton's New Town, and Old Town remained a small residential and visitor district. The modern community planning area includes the historic core, residential slopes rising toward Mission Hills, and a growing number of apartment and condominium buildings near the Old Town Transit Center.

Housing in Old Town

Housing in Old Town is limited and varied. On the hillsides east of the state park heading toward Mission Hills, streets are lined with modest single-family houses built between the 1910s and 1950s, frequently on oddly shaped lots that climb the slope. Several multifamily buildings from the 1970s and 1980s contain condominiums and townhomes, and newer mid-rise residential projects have been built near the transit center and along the Pacific Highway and Congress Street corridors. Because much of the flat land is taken up by the state park, hotels and commercial uses, sales of detached homes are infrequent. Properties fall within the Old Town San Diego planned district, where exterior changes and new construction are subject to design standards.

What shifts value here

Proximity to the transit center, which links the Trolley, Coaster and Amtrak, supports demand for condos and townhomes, while traffic from Interstate 5 and Interstate 8 and visitor activity around San Diego Avenue bring noise and congestion. Hillside homes with views over Mission Bay or the river channel sell at a premium compared with flat lots near commercial uses. The planned district sets height and design limits that restrict how much new building a parcel can support. Parts of the lower area near the river lie within mapped flood zones, which can raise insurance costs. Airport operations to the south add aircraft noise on some blocks.

Valuation approach

Few detached sales occur in Old Town in any given year, so the comparable search often extends into Mission Hills, the Morena area and Point Loma, with location adjustments supported by paired sales where possible. Condominiums are compared within the same project first and then with transit-oriented buildings of similar age. Adjustments address views, freeway exposure, parking and lot slope. Owners should gather any planned district approvals for alterations, permits for additions, flood zone determinations and HOA documents. For mixed-use or live-work properties, information about commercial use and income helps define the highest and best use.

Local question

With so few house sales in Old Town, how can an appraiser support a value for an estate?

The appraiser widens the search in time and distance, drawing on older Old Town sales and recent sales in adjoining hillside areas with similar views and age. Market condition adjustments bring older sales forward to the effective date, and location adjustments are tested with paired sales. The report explains the reasoning so that attorneys, heirs and the court can follow how the limited data was handled.
